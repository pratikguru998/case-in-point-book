## Case 6 · Stuck {#stuck}

<CaseMeta level={2} format="Partner case" types={['Strategy', 'Production capacity']} pages="301–306" />

**Prompt (read aloud):** *Our client is the third-largest U.S. peanut-butter brand, selling **120M jars** a year; the fourth-place brand is only two share points behind. Sales: supermarkets and convenience stores 60%, warehouse clubs 25%, and one giant discount retailer **15%**. That retailer is replacing our brand with its **own private label**, but it wants **us to make it**: same recipe, same jar, different label. (1) What strategic issues should we weigh? (2) Run the numbers: does it make sense?*

<details>
<summary>🎙️ Interviewer's data bank</summary>

- If asked: peanut-butter sales have been **flat** for two years and will stay flat. The retailer is replacing the number-three brand in many categories with its own label.
- Part 1: MBA candidates should find 6–7 issues, undergrads 4–5. They should **state the issue, then ask the question** behind it.
- Part 2 data: rivals sell at **$3.99** a jar in supermarkets; ours sells at **$3.69**. Plant capacity **150M jars** a year. Profit per jar: supermarkets **$1.20**, clubs **$1.00**, the retailer today (as our brand) **$0.50**. New offer: **one-year** contract, **50M jars at $0.25** profit each.
- Then, whatever fix they propose for the capacity gap, say **"No. What else?"** and cut them off once or twice. Watch how they react.

</details>

<details>
<summary>✅ Solution & scorecard</summary>

**Part 1: strategic issues**

| Issue | The question behind it |
|---|---|
| Profit | Will the contract pay? This retailer is famous for squeezing margins. |
| Brand equity | If shoppers learn the private label is our product, does our brand suffer? |
| Capacity | How big is the contract, and can we make it? |
| Cannibalisation | Will we steal sales from our own brand? |
| Market share | We drop to 4th either way: the private label doesn't count as our share. |
| Dependency | The retailer could become ~20%+ of our volume. Risky with any one customer. |
| Other private labels | Once we do one, will other chains ask too, or replace us? |
| The "no" scenario | Lose 15% of volume, idle lines, lay people off. |

**Part 2: the numbers**

| | Calculation | Profit |
|---|---|---|
| Old deal | 120M × 15% = 18M jars × $0.50 | $9.0M |
| New deal | 50M jars × $0.25 | **$12.5M** (+$3.5M) |

**The catch: capacity.** 120M − 18M = 102M of our own + 50M = **152M jars vs 150M capacity**, so we're 2M short.

Ideas the interviewer rejects: outsource (contract says we make it; quality control) · new line or acquisition (long-term fix for a one-year deal) · overtime (already running three shifts, seven days a week) · ship 48M (contract says 50M) · take 2M from the clubs (loses $2M of the $3.5M gain).

**The one right answer: raise the supermarket price by 20¢.** We're still below rivals ($3.89 vs $3.99) and profit rises to $1.40 a jar. Analysts expect volume to drop 10%:

| | Jars | Profit per jar | Profit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Before | 72M (60% of 120M) | $1.20 | $86.4M |
| After | ≈65M (−10%) | $1.40 | **≈$91M** |

About 7M fewer jars (which frees the capacity) **and** about $5M more profit.

**What really counts:** how fast they spot the **capacity** issue, and how they react to repeated "No, what else?". Fewer than 10% of candidates find the price increase; **staying calm under rejection matters more** than finding it.

</details>

## Case 7 · Nerves of Steel {#nerves-of-steel}

<CaseMeta level={2} format="Partner case" types={['Strategy', 'Numbers', 'Time value of money']} pages="307–311" />

**Prompt (read aloud):** *Our client is the second-largest U.S. maker of steel filing cabinets and desks. Its four-year rolled-steel contract (signed in Year 4) **expires at the end of Year 7**. The CFO asks: should we **stockpile two years of steel at the old Year 4 price** before the contract ends, or **sign a new contract at Year 8 prices**? Assume we use **12,000 tons a year**.*

<details>
<summary>🎙️ Interviewer's data bank</summary>

Give these as the candidate asks (allow time for them to work out what they need):

| Item | Value |
|---|---|
| Year 4 contract price | **$600/ton** |
| Current (Year 7) market price | **$810/ton** |
| Amount to stockpile | **24,000 tons** (2 years) |
| Interest rate | **5%** (use `FV = PV × (1 + i)^n`) |
| Storage | **$50/ton per year**, paid at the start of each year |
| Price forecast / economy | Hand over a historical price chart: it jumps around and mostly just anchors Year 8 near $810 |

Make them **commit to a Year 8 price** (e.g. $850) based on the economy, especially the big steel users: **cars, aircraft and appliances** (tied to housing starts). Big points for a **final slide**. They should also realise that stockpiling means **signing a new contract in Year 10**, so they need a Year 10 price too (e.g. $900).

</details>

<details>
<summary>✅ Solution & scorecard</summary>

**Final slide (draw it at the start):**

| | Option A: sign in Year 8 | Option B: stockpile, sign in Year 10 |
|---|---|---|
| Steel price | Year 8 forecast (e.g. $850) | $600 now; Year 10 forecast later (e.g. $900) |
| Steel cost, Years 8–9 | … | … |
| Storage | — | … |
| Cost of money | — | … |
| Exposure after Year 9 | Locked in at the Year 8 price | Must sign at the Year 10 price |
| **Decision** | | |

**One way to work it** (with $850 and $900 as the assumed prices):

| Years 8–9 | A: contract at $850 | B: stockpile at $600 |
|---|---|---|
| Steel | 24,000 × $850 = **$20.4M** | 24,000 × $600 = **$14.4M** (paid upfront) |
| Cost of money on the upfront payment | — | 14.4M × (1.05² − 1) ≈ **$1.5M** |
| Storage (start of Year 8: 24K tons; start of Year 9: 12K tons) | — | $1.2M + $0.6M = **$1.8M** (plus a little interest) |
| **Total** | **$20.4M** | **≈$17.7M** |

B saves about **$2.7M** over Years 8–9, but then pays $900 instead of $850 from Year 10: about **$0.6M more per year**. Over a four-year horizon B still comes out roughly $1.5M ahead; with a much higher Year 10 forecast it may not. The answer depends on **your price forecasts**, which is why the interviewer forces you to commit to them.

**Scorecard:** knows the current economy · realises a new contract is needed in Year 10 · builds the **final slide**, fills it in, and **turns it toward the interviewer** to walk through the decision.

</details>

## Case 8 · GPS App {#gps-app}

<CaseMeta level={2} format="Partner case" types={['Market sizing', 'Breakeven', 'Pricing', 'Profit']} pages="312–315" />

**Prompt (read aloud):** *Two college students built an app that lets people download **celebrity voices** for their phone's GPS directions. Find the U.S. **market size**, the **breakeven** number of downloads, the **price**, and first-year **profit**.*

<details>
<summary>🎙️ Interviewer's data bank</summary>

- No other objectives. First to market; patent pending, but expect copycats within about **a year**.
- Watch whether they work out **price before market size** and draw a final slide straight away.
- **Costs:** fixed **$500,000**. Variable: **one-third of the price** goes to the app store, plus a **$0.50 royalty** per download to the celebrity.
- Comparable products (songs, ringtones, games) sell for free up to $9.99; songs and ringtones about $1.29.
- Sizing: 320M people, 80-year life span, even age spread. Drivers aged roughly **16–36** are the likeliest buyers. Steer them to about **5M buyers × 2 voices = 10M downloads** in year one.

</details>

<details>
<summary>✅ Solution & scorecard</summary>

**Final slide, ordered the smart way:** Price → Market size → Breakeven → Profit.

| | Working | Answer |
|---|---|---|
| **Price** | Comparable apps $1–10; pick an easy number. At $3: $1 to the app store, $0.50 royalty → **$1.50 margin** | **$3** |
| **Market size** | Smartphone-owning drivers aged ~16–36, by age group → ~5M buyers × 2 voices | **10M downloads** |
| **Breakeven** | $500,000 ÷ $1.50 | **≈333,000 downloads** |
| **Profit, year 1** | 10M × $1.50 − $0.5M | **$14.5M** |

**Summary = the final slide**, turned toward the interviewer.

**Mark of a good discussion:** did they set the **price first**? Most candidates size the market first. That's acceptable but unremarkable. For a new product with no direct competitor, price comes first.

</details>

## Case 9 · Finding Fulfillment {#finding-fulfillment}

<CaseMeta level={2} format="Partner case" types={['Outsourcing', 'Strategy by numbers']} pages="316–321" />

**Prompt (read aloud):** *Our client is an online toy store with one warehouse outside Boston; it ships to customers via an express carrier. Should it **outsource fulfillment** to a third-party fulfillment company? (1) What are the pros and cons? (2) Does it make financial sense?*

<details>
<summary>🎙️ Interviewer's data bank</summary>

- No other objectives. No elaborate structure needed: pros and cons, then the numbers. They should **ask for a moment**, then give all pros, then all cons. If they improvise, cut them off: "I get it. What else?"
- Key insight to listen for: it's a toy business, so about **80% of sales happen in the last two months**. The warehouse sits **three-quarters empty for ten months**.
- Numbers (we've picked a vendor):
  - 250 orders a day × 20 days × **10 months**; **2,000 orders a day** × 20 days × **2 holiday months**. Average toy **$20**.
  - Warehouse rent, utilities and equipment **$75,000** a year; insurance **$9,000**.
  - 4 full-time staff with benefits **$200,000** a year; **30 holiday temps** at $10/hour × 8 hours × 20 days × 2 months.
  - Vendor storage **$0.45 per cubic foot** per month: 10,000 cu ft for 10 months, 100,000 cu ft for 2 months.
  - **$2 restocking fee** per returned item; **3%** return rate.
  - The client charges customers a **$1 handling fee** per package; if outsourced, it pays the vendor **$1 per package**.
- **Force a decision**, then say "Let me tell you why you're wrong."

</details>

<details>
<summary>✅ Solution & scorecard</summary>

**Volumes:** 5,000 × 10 = 50,000 + 40,000 × 2 = 80,000 → **130,000 packages**, × $20 = **$2.6M revenue**.

| In-house | | Outsourced | |
|---|---|---|---|
| Full-time staff | $200,000 | Storage, 10 months: 0.45 × 10,000 × 10 | $45,000 |
| Holiday temps: 30 × $10 × 8 × 20 × 2 | $96,000 | Storage, 2 months: 0.45 × 100,000 × 2 | $90,000 |
| Rent, utilities, equipment | $75,000 | $1 per package to the vendor | $130,000 |
| Insurance | $9,000 | Returns: 3% × 130,000 × $2 | $7,800 |
| **Less** $1 handling fee collected | −$130,000 | | |
| **Net** | **$250,000** | **Total** | **$272,800** |

In-house is **$22,800 cheaper**. Now **put that in perspective**.

**If they choose in-house, argue for outsourcing:** $22,800 is under **1%** of revenue (about 1,100 extra toys covers it) and frees management to sell more · the $1 fee can still be **passed on** to customers · shipping rates may be cheaper · the industry is moving to **free holiday shipping**, which would wipe out ~$80,000 of that fee income · worried about a bad vendor? **use two vendors** in year one, then pick one.

**If they choose outsourcing, argue for in-house:** control of the brand and delivery times · an empty warehouse for ten months is an **opportunity**: rent out space, fulfil for other companies, or sell spring and summer products (patio, pool, garden) for **seasonal balance**.

**Good:** no math slips, tidy notes turned toward the interviewer, a clear recommendation, **defended without getting defensive**. **Great:** puts the $22,800 in perspective, passes the fee on, sees the free-shipping trend, finds new uses for the warehouse. (Out of ~200 candidates, almost nobody raised the free-shipping point.)

</details>

## Case 10 · KBO Appliances {#kbo}

<CaseMeta level={2} format="Partner case" types={['Entering a new market', 'Pricing', 'Market sizing']} pages="322–326" />

**Prompt (read aloud):** *A U.S. kitchen-appliance maker's stock fell from **$34 to $30** after critics called its new line "pedestrian". The CEO has ordered a redesign, and she also wants to enter the **college market** with a **mini-fridge + microwave combo**. What's the market size, how would you price it, and how much profit can we expect?*

<details>
<summary>🎙️ Interviewer's data bank</summary>

- They should say the stock fell **about 12%**. No other objectives.
- Start by asking: "What do you want to know?" Aim to cover all eight issues on the final slide below. Anything they don't raise, ask about, then give them the data.
- **Market sizing** (general population): 320M ÷ 80 = 4M per age year; college age covers about **5 years** → 20M; **8M full-time** students → 2M per class year. Freshmen all live in dorms, mostly with a roommate → **1M units**; all other years together → about **1M more** (students keep units year to year). The **biggest buyers are universities**, which buy and rent the units to students. Whatever they get, use **2M units a year**.
- **Competition:** every existing product looks the same: a white mini-fridge with a microwave on top. Ask how they'd differentiate, then go with an **energy-efficient fridge with a distinctive cow-print design**.
- The rest of the data bank (price range, costs, expected share) is printed as a table on PDF p. 324. Read it from your copy, or make up reasonable figures.
- Key reveal: the CEO wants to introduce students to the brand's **quality** so they buy bigger appliances later in life (long-term greed). That points to a price at the **low end** of the range.

</details>

<details>
<summary>✅ Solution & scorecard</summary>

**Final slide: eight issues, in a sensible order:**

| # | Issue | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Why enter? | Brand-building with future customers → affects pricing |
| 2 | Market size | ~2M units a year |
| 3 | Customers | **Universities** buy most units (energy efficiency matters to them) |
| 4 | Competition & differentiation | Identical white boxes → energy-efficient + distinctive design |
| 5 | Price | **Low end** of the range, given the long-term goal |
| 6 | Expected share | Needs the price first |
| 7 | Costs | From the data bank |
| 8 | Profit | Needs the share first → units × margin |

<KeyIdea>Everything is linked: you can't estimate **share** until you know the **price**, and you can't compute **profit** until you know the **share**. Does the candidate see the big picture?</KeyIdea>

**Good interview:** quantifies the stock drop · takes into account *why* the CEO wants to enter · no math slips · makes the chart · summary = walking through the chart.

**Red flag:** proposing a **price war** as a response. Most firms see it as a knee-jerk reaction.

</details>
